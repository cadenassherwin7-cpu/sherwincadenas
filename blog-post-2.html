From Risk Identification to Risk Treatment: What Our Risk Assessment Fieldwork Taught Us About Information Security

By: Sherwin Cadenas Information Security Fieldwork Group October 2026 Estimated reading time: 7–10 minutes
ASSET
RISK
CONTROL
REVIEW

A simplified view of the assessment cycle: understand the asset, evaluate the risk, choose a treatment, and review the result.

Information security can look very technical from a classroom perspective. During our fieldwork, however, we found that security decisions are closely connected to people, procedures, physical records, organizational activities, and the everyday choices that determine how information is handled.

Introduction — From Classroom Concepts to Real-World Risk

Our risk assessment fieldwork gave us a chance to move beyond definitions and apply the concepts we had been studying to an actual organizational setting. Instead of treating risk as a list of possible cyber threats, we had to think about what the organization actually needs to protect, what could affect those assets, what controls were already present, and what should happen next.

That experience changed our understanding of information security. We began to see that a secure environment is not created by one tool or one policy. It is shaped by the combination of technology, people, processes, access decisions, physical safeguards, and continuous review.

1. Understanding Risk in Practice

One of the first lessons we learned was that risk assessment starts with the asset. It is difficult to judge a risk without understanding what could be affected and why that asset matters to the organization.

During the fieldwork, we considered examples of digital and physical information assets such as workstations, cloud-based records, paper documents, removable storage, forms, and administrative information. Each asset has a role in daily operations, and each may have different confidentiality, integrity, or availability concerns.

Good risk assessment connects a possible security event to the asset, process, and people that could actually be affected.

This helped us move away from asking only whether a system was “secure.” Instead, we started asking more useful questions: What are we protecting? What could happen? How serious would the result be? What is already being done to reduce the exposure?

2. How We Identified and Assessed Risks

Our assessment process followed a simple sequence. We identified an asset, considered a relevant risk source or scenario, documented existing controls, evaluated impact and probability, and then used the resulting assessment to think about treatment.

The distinction between impact and probability was especially useful. Impact asks how serious the consequences could be, while probability considers how likely the scenario is to occur. Looking at both helped us avoid automatically treating every possible problem as equally important.

Risk scoring framework Risk Score = Impact × Probability
1–3Low Risk
4–5Moderate Risk
6–9Significant Risk

The calculation itself is simple, but the discussion behind the numbers was more important. Our group had to agree on how the assessment criteria applied to each scenario and make sure that the ratings were based on the situation rather than assumptions.

3. How We Prioritized the Risks

Risk prioritization helped us understand where attention should be focused. A scenario with a serious potential consequence may deserve attention even if it is not expected to happen often. In the same way, a frequent event may not represent the same level of concern if its effect is minor.

This showed us why a consistent method matters. A risk score does not make the decision automatically, but it gives the group a shared starting point for discussing what the organization should do.

4. From Risk Assessment to Risk Treatment

Central lesson

Risk treatment turns assessment into action.

Identifying a risk is only part of the process. The next question is what reasonable response should follow from the assessment.

Our fieldwork helped us understand the difference between accepting a risk and mitigating it. Acceptance means that the organization decides to live with the identified level of risk, usually because it falls within an established tolerance or because further treatment is not proportionate to the situation. Mitigation means applying measures intended to reduce the likelihood or severity of a possible loss.

This distinction was important because accepting a risk does not mean ignoring it. An organization can accept a low-level risk while still maintaining existing safeguards and considering practical improvements.

Our group also learned that treatment decisions should be connected to the evidence gathered during assessment. If the risk is significant, stronger or additional controls may be justified. If the risk is low and existing controls are reasonable, maintaining those controls and monitoring the situation may be appropriate.

5. Choosing Appropriate Security Controls

Another lesson was that effective controls do not always require expensive technology. A control is useful when it addresses the risk and can actually be followed in the organization's daily environment.

Examples of practical controls include individual user accounts, stronger authentication, periodic access-permission reviews, secure physical storage, controlled handling of removable storage, reliable backups, and restricted editing rights for critical records.

These measures may appear simple, but their value comes from consistent implementation. A well-defined access rule that nobody follows is not an effective safeguard. Likewise, a backup plan that is never tested cannot provide the same confidence as a backup process that is regularly verified.

6. The Human Side of Information Security

Our fieldwork also reinforced that people are part of the security environment. Employees make decisions about access, documents, passwords, devices, communication, and procedures every day.

Because of this, security awareness and clear responsibility matter. People need to understand not only what a rule says, but why it exists and what could happen if it is ignored. Team communication also matters when responsibilities overlap or when a process changes.

Information security is not only about protecting systems. It is also about helping people make responsible decisions around information.

7. Keeping Risk From Creeping Back

Another important lesson was that risk management cannot stop when an assessment form is completed. Organizations change. Staff responsibilities change, systems are updated, new workflows appear, and new threats can develop.

For that reason, risk should be reviewed periodically. Permission checks, backup verification, monitoring, and reassessment can help determine whether existing safeguards still support the organization's needs.

Thinking about review changed our view of security from a one-time project into an ongoing process. A control that is useful today still needs attention tomorrow.

8. What Our Group Would Improve Next Time

If we conducted the assessment again, we would prepare more detailed interview questions before the fieldwork and organize our evidence collection more systematically. We would also spend more time making sure that every group member interpreted the assessment criteria in the same way before assigning ratings.

Another improvement would be to document the relationship between an asset, risk scenario, existing control, and proposed treatment more clearly. Doing that from the beginning would make the final analysis easier to explain and reduce the chance of leaving out important context.

Most importantly, we would continue to protect the assessment information itself. Fieldwork can reveal details about how an organization operates, so academic reporting should avoid exposing credentials, personal information, or specific weaknesses that could create unnecessary security risks.

Conclusion

Our risk assessment fieldwork changed our understanding of information security from a mostly technical topic into a practical management and responsibility issue. We learned how to identify assets, describe risk scenarios, assess impact and probability, consider existing controls, choose treatment, and review whether safeguards remain useful.

The experience also showed us that effective security does not necessarily mean eliminating every possible risk. It means understanding what could go wrong, estimating its significance using consistent criteria, applying appropriate controls, and continuing to review the situation.

As students preparing for professional work in computing and information security, the fieldwork gave us a more realistic view of what security practice looks like. It is about informed decisions, responsible information handling, practical controls, teamwork, and continuous attention to risk.
